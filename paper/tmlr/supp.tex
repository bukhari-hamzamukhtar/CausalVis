\section{Protocol}
\label{app:prereg}

\paragraph{Pre-registration.}
Before the fresh test set was scored, we wrote down the inputs, the sets, the selection rule, the systems to be scored and the statistics to be reported.
Settings were chosen on VAL-A only; TEST-A and TEST-B were then scored once per listed system, and nothing was changed afterwards.
The listed systems were the learned engine, the textbook laws, straight lines, straight lines with friction, no physics, seven one-change ablations of the learned engine, nine other checkpoints of the learned model, six retrained seeds, a vision-language baseline, the other three question types, and the learned engine with the dataset's own question programs as a check of the parser.
Anything else reported in the paper is labelled as a separate analysis (the evidence audit, the explanatory but-for test and the key-consistency check).

\paragraph{History of the sets.}
TEST-A served as the test set of earlier versions of the engine, so some design choices may have been influenced by it.
For this reason we added TEST-B, 898 CLEVRER training videos that no model had been trained on and that had never been scored, and named it the primary test set.
Every significant comparison of the main paper keeps its direction on TEST-B alone (\cref{app:full}); the entry correction, not significant on the pooled sets, is significant on TEST-B alone ($z=-2.2$).

\paragraph{Selection on VAL-A.}
For each simulator we swept the hand-off look-ahead $L \in \{1,3,6,12\}$, five collision detectors and the extension $E \in \{0,10,20,30,45,60\}$, and kept the setting with the most correct options (ties broken by correct questions).
\cref{tab:val} lists the chosen settings.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Intervals resample videos, because the options of one question and the questions of one video are not independent: 2,000 resamples with a fixed seed.
Paired comparisons use McNemar's test on the options both systems answer, reported as $z = (b - c)/\sqrt{b+c}$, where $b$ and $c$ count the options only one of the two systems gets right; the same is done for questions.
For the pooled sets the set name is part of the video identifier, so that the bootstrap and the grouping of questions stay correct.

\section{Perception and question parsing}
\label{app:perception}
\label{app:parser}

\paragraph{Tracks from detections.}
We use the Mask R-CNN detections released with NS-DR for all 20,000 CLEVRER videos, each with predicted colour, material and shape.
Detections of the same colour and shape closer than 8 pixels are merged; two objects may share colour and shape only if two detections appear at least 20 pixels apart in 3 or more frames; tracks shorter than 5 frames are dropped; gaps of up to 5 frames are interpolated; the material is a score-weighted majority vote.
Objects are identified by colour, material and shape together, because 21\% of CLEVRER videos contain two objects that share colour and shape.

\paragraph{Camera.}
The camera published for CLEVR scenes does not match CLEVRER.
We recovered it by rendering the convex hulls of the three shapes and fitting camera position and focal length to the detected masks, which raised the median silhouette IoU from 0.57 to 0.93.
Mask centres are lifted to the table plane at the object's centre height.

\paragraph{Question parser.}
Questions are parsed by templates learned from CLEVRER's training questions: each template has typed slots that are filled by voting over the training programs.
On the validation questions the parser reproduces the dataset's programs exactly for 100\% of counterfactual and predictive questions, 99.995\% of explanatory questions and 99.80\% of descriptive questions.
With the dataset's programs instead of parsed ones, the scores change by at most 0.2 points.

\paragraph{Training.}
The base model was trained for 30 epochs on the tracks of 11,182 training videos with a rollout loss whose horizon grew from 3 to 20 frames (AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch 16, gradient clipping at 1.0; 5.2 hours).
The final model was fine-tuned for 4 epochs at horizon 20 with voxel contacts inside the rollout (learning rate $3 \times 10^{-4}$; 2.9 hours), keeping the epoch with the lowest validation rollout error.
Object radii are measured from the masks and kept fixed.
Six seeds repeat the fine-tune. Seeds 1 and 2 were stopped after two of four epochs by a power loss, and in both the best epoch was the first, as in the original run; seeds 3 to 6 ran all four epochs on a free hosted CPU session and their best epochs were the fourth, second, third and third.
Their validation rollout errors at 20 frames are 0.0214, 0.0253, 0.0231 and 0.0239 against the released model's 0.0230, but the trainer draws its validation windows from the seed, so these are not measured on identical windows; relative to its own starting point seed 3 improves 22\% where the released model improved 13\%.
Across the five checkpoints that have a validation number the correlation between rollout error and options correct is $-0.76$, with the benchmark spanning 0.5 points while the rollout error spans 18\%.
The seven checkpoints of this recipe score $90.3 \pm 0.2$ per option and $71.8 \pm 0.6$ per question.

\paragraph{Vision-language baseline.}
We also asked a general vision-language model the same options, to see what the task looks like to a system that watches the video and answers from what it sees.
Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct receives 16 frames spread evenly over the clip and the list of events, and is asked whether each event happens once the named object is removed; decoding is greedy, and the polarity of questions that ask which event will \emph{not} happen is applied when scoring, so the number does not depend on how the model handles negation.
The same run was repeated with the frames withheld.

With the frames the model is indistinguishable from never predicting a collision (54.4 against 54.1), and it is 35 points below the engine on the same options (paired: the engine is right where the model is wrong 1,362 times, the reverse 177 times, $z = -30.2$).
It answers ``no'' to every event of 86\% of questions when it sees the frames and ``yes'' to every event of 70\% of them when it does not, so what changes its answer is whether images are present rather than which object was removed.
Two of 1,859 replies could not be read and were counted as ``no''.
Concurrent work measures the same effect on a different subset and with much larger models: a blind vision-language model answers 50.1 of counterfactual options, GPT-4o 56.5 and GPT-5.5 76.7, against a 50.0 chance level (PhysMind, Table 1, cited in the main paper).
Our 54.4 for a 7B open model sits where that range predicts, and the blind number is close to the 48.8 we measure.
This is a baseline and not a fair comparison with the published systems: it is one prompt of one open model with no fine-tuning on this dataset, and a system built around such a model could do better.
It does say that the frames alone, read by a strong general model, do not answer these questions.

\paragraph{Textbook laws.}
Fitted on training tracks: cubes and cylinders slide with a constant deceleration of 0.00008 per frame; spheres roll and lose 0.3\% (metal) or 0.2\% (rubber) of their speed per frame; collisions apply an impulse along the line of centres with restitution 0.2, a metal to rubber mass ratio of 1.00 and contact friction 0.05.

\paragraph{Engine.}
Positions are in scene units divided by 6.
The calibrated distance rule counts a collision when the centre distance is below $1.2(r_i + r_j) + 0.03$; the path-change rule when the surfaces are within 0.03 and one object's velocity changes by at least 0.002 within 6 frames; contacts closer than 6 frames are merged.
An object that leaves the table ($|x| > 0.95$) stops interacting.

\paragraph{Other question types.}
Descriptive: calibrated rule with the collision onset moved to the closest approach within 6 frames; an object is moving if its speed exceeds 0.001. Explanatory: the same detector and CLEVRER's chain rule. Predictive: the plain distance rule ($r_i + r_j + 0.02$) and 60 frames of simulation past the end. All chosen on VAL-A.

\paragraph{Compute.}
One laptop CPU (Intel i5-8350U, 4 cores, 8 threads, 7.9 GB of memory), no GPU, for every stage.
Answering one counterfactual question (building its worlds and reading every option) takes 2.24 seconds with the learned model, 1.36 with the laws, 1.40 with straight lines and 1.04 without physics, measured on 40 TEST-A questions with one process, one thread and nothing else running (start-up excluded).
Training took 8.1 hours in total.

\section{Evidence audit}
\label{app:events}

An answer's deciding event is the first collision of the asked pair in the counterfactual world, or its absence.
It is \emph{recorded} when both objects still follow the recording at that frame and \emph{simulated} otherwise.
A recorded collision is confirmed if the annotation has a collision of the same pair within a tolerance; a recorded absence is confirmed if the annotation has no collision of the pair in the whole video (the part after the video end is simulated and not checked).

\section{Explanatory questions and CLEVRER-Humans}
\label{app:explanatory}

\paragraph{But-for test on CLEVRER.}
For each option we locate the target collision and the candidate cause on the recorded events.
A candidate ``presence of $Z$'' or ``$Z$'s entrance'' removes $Z$; a candidate collision removes whichever of its objects is not in the target collision.
A candidate that shares both objects with the target cannot be removed without removing the target and keeps the chain-rule answer, as do candidates that could not be resolved.
The extension past the video end for the target check was chosen on VAL-A (+0 frames: 76.8\%; +10: 76.4\%; +30: 75.3\%).

\paragraph{Is it the same collision?}
CLEVRER's counterfactual answer carries no time, so ``$X$ and $Y$ still collide without $Z$'' could refer to a different collision.
For the 113 cases in which the key calls $Z$ responsible and the pair still collides without $Z$, the pair collides exactly once in the recorded video in all 113.
In the engine's world without $Z$, the first collision of the pair lies within 10 frames of the recorded one in 56 cases, more than 10 frames earlier in 23, more than 10 frames later in 14, and does not happen in 20.

\paragraph{CLEVRER-Humans.}
CLEVRER-Humans describes events in free-form sentences.
We linked each sentence to objects and a frame with a language model (gpt-oss-120b through the Groq service, temperature 0, outputs cached and released), then asked whether the cause event is responsible for the effect event.
The but-for test removes the cause's object and checks whether the effect still happens in the simulated world; the chain rule applies CLEVRER's rule to our recorded events.
The rule was chosen on 102 grounded training videos (240 options: but-for 64.6\%, chain rule 58.8\%, always yes 52.1\%).
On the validation split (216 questions, 432 options) the but-for test answers 64.4\% of options and 43.5\% of questions, the chain rule 60.9\% and 39.4\%, always yes 50.7\% and 30.1\%, the best published model 54.0\% and 34.2\%, and people 84.5\% and 71.4\%.
Of the 183 validation videos, 150 lie in our world model's training split; only their trajectories were used, never any label.

\section{ComPhy}
\label{app:comphy}

ComPhy hides mass and charge. Each target video comes with four reference videos of the same objects.
We take object tracks from the annotation (visible frames of the first five seconds only), infer each object's mass (light or heavy) and charge (positive, negative or none) by searching for the assignment whose simulations best fit the target and reference videos, and simulate interventions and the future with laws fitted on training annotations (friction, a charge constant of 0.138, contact and restitution).
Settings were chosen on 300 training scenes: reference videos on, counterfactual extension +0 frames, predictive extension +50 frames.
The hidden mass is recovered exactly in 98\% of mass scenes; whether an object is charged in 99.6\% of charge scenes, but the exact signs of all charges in only 11\%.
The validation set has 2 options per question and the test set mostly 4, so per-question accuracy is not comparable; ours is 66.7\% (counterfactual) and 74.1\% (predictive).
371 of 15,968 options name an object we could not resolve and are answered no.

\paragraph{Evidence audit on ComPhy.}
ComPhy's annotation includes the frames after the end of each video (frames 125 to 174), so the event that decides a predictive answer can be checked.
On 400 validation scenes (1,597 predictive options whose two objects we resolve), 1,411 answers are correct.
Of these, 589 are decided by a simulated collision that matches a real future collision of the same pair within 5 frames (553 within 3 frames, 603 within 10), 789 by an absence of collision that matches the real future, and 33 by a simulated collision with no real counterpart within 5 frames: 97.7\% of the correct answers rest on a real event.
Of the 186 wrong answers, 152 miss a real future collision, 8 invent one, and 26 say no collision where the annotated future window also shows none, which suggests collisions after frame 174 in the answer key.
Counted per asked pair, the first simulated future collision is real in 93.5\% of cases (589 of 630) and finds 76.9\% of the real ones (589 of 766).

\section{CoPhy}
\label{app:cophy}
